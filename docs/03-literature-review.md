# Literature Review

## Critical Literature Review

Research on AI-based career guidance shows good results in some technical evaluations, but it is less clear whether these systems actually help people make better career decisions. Sarmurzin et al. [1] reviewed 43 studies on university career counselling and found limited evidence of long-term benefits. Manganello et al. [2] reached a similar conclusion in their review of 21 studies. Many systems were evaluated through model performance, while their usefulness for students received less attention. For the proposed platform, a relevant recommendation alone would not prove that the career guidance is useful.

There is already considerable research on recommendation methods. Lahoud et al. [3] compared five approaches for recommending university majors and career domains. Their hybrid system gave good results using data from Lebanese university graduates. However, the study focused on educational choices rather than matching a person's CV with professional requirements. Çelik Ertuğrul and Bitirim [4] also show that career planning and training recommendations based on skill gaps have been studied before. Therefore, the planned project does not treat these functions as completely new. The main idea is to connect them in one process, from the user's skills to a suitable profession and further development steps.

One of the first problems is extracting skills correctly. Senger et al. [8] explain that skill identification, standardisation and classification are different tasks, although some papers use these terms interchangeably. SkillSpan by Zhang et al. [11] provides an annotated dataset and shows better extraction results with models adapted to job postings. But the extracted phrases were not linked to individual ESCO identifiers. Nguyen et al. [9] tested LLM-based extraction on six datasets. Their best GPT-3.5 prompting setting performed worse than a supervised model under strict evaluation. GPT-4 was tested only on smaller subsets. LLMs may recognise complex expressions, but they can also change the original wording or miss skills. Another limitation is that these experiments mainly used job advertisements. Similar performance on uploaded CVs cannot be assumed without testing it.

For matching profiles with occupations, semantic similarity is a possible approach. Reimers and Gurevych [12] introduced Sentence-BERT, which makes comparing sentence embeddings much more efficient. CareerBERT by Rosenberger et al. [5] uses related methods to recommend standardised ESCO occupations from resume information. The results depend not only on the language model but also on how occupations are represented. For example, vectors based on job advertisements gave better ranking results in one experiment, but were available for fewer occupations. The main ranking experiments used job advertisements as substitutes for resumes. The separate human evaluation involved only five real resumes and ten HR experts. Such results are useful for choosing a method, but a high similarity score should not be interpreted as a person's actual chance of getting a job.

ESCO can help make skill names consistent, especially when the same competence is described in different words. Zhang et al. [10] trained ESCOXLM-R using taxonomy data in 27 languages, but evaluated it on datasets in only four languages. Kavas et al. [6] worked with Spanish candidate and vacancy information and mapped extracted skills to English ESCO labels. Their enhanced Claude-based skill selection had higher recall and F1 than its tested baseline, although precision became lower. These studies support using a standard skills taxonomy, but they do not confirm that the same models will work correctly with Kazakh or Russian CVs. This would need separate testing.

The explanation of recommendations is another part that should not be ignored. Tang et al. [7] discuss explainability at the data, model and output levels. For the planned system, it would be more useful to show which skills match a profession and which requirements are not covered, instead of giving only a percentage. However, a skill that is absent from a CV is not necessarily missing from the person's knowledge. The issue might be incomplete profile information or an extraction error. Because of this, extracted skills should be editable before they are used for matching and skill-gap analysis.

The development roadmap will also need reliable information. Lewis et al. [13] showed that retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) can produce more factual and specific text than a model working without retrieved documents in their tested tasks. Still, their study was not about career roadmaps, and retrieval did not remove incorrect generation completely. Es et al. [14] proposed RAGAS to evaluate how well generated answers follow the retrieved context and answer the question. Its results were promising, but the experiments were based on general questions, not professional development plans. Therefore, automatic checks may help detect unsupported statements, while the relevance and feasibility of recommended steps should also be assessed by people with experience in the field.

The reviewed papers cover many parts of the planned system separately: skill extraction, ESCO mapping, occupation matching, explainability and text generation. What remains to be tested in this project is how these parts work together for a person who may have a short or incomplete CV. The proposed platform will combine an editable skills profile, ranked professions, skill-gap analysis and a development roadmap based on retrieved sources. Each stage should be evaluated separately, and the recommendations should be compared with a simpler baseline. This would provide more meaningful results than reporting a matching percentage without checking whether it is correct or understandable.

(2025) | Multilingual skill extraction and vacancy–candidate matching through ESCO. | Combines several skill-extraction methods, LLM-based selection, ESCO mapping and a knowledge graph. | Enhanced skill selection increased F1 from 0.43 to 0.48 and recall from 0.34 to 0.50, while precision decreased from 0.58 to 0.46. | Matching used synthetic candidate experiences. Some matching results use different similarity measures, so direct comparison is limited. | Supports treating extraction, taxonomy linking and matching as separate steps, with human validation. |
| [7] Tang et al. (2025) | Explainability in person–job recommendation. | Review of 85 studies, grouping explanation methods into data, model and output levels. | Explains different ways to show why a job was recommended and the trade-offs between clarity and model performance. | The review does not establish one best method for all systems, and its search and appraisal have limitations. | Supports showing matched and missing requirements and testing whether users understand the explanation. |
| [8] Senger et al. | [9] Nguyen et al. GPT-4 was tested on smaller subsets. LLMs sometimes handle combined skill phrases better. | Generated text may not follow the required format. Results from job ads cannot be directly applied to CVs. | Suggests checking extracted skills against manual labels and comparing LLM extraction with a simpler or supervised approach. |
| [10] Zhang et al. (2023) | Multilingual language models for job-market NLP tasks. | ESCOXLM-R is further trained on ESCO data in 27 languages and evaluated on nine datasets in four languages. | Outperformed earlier reported results on six of nine datasets, particularly for shorter skill spans. | Evaluation does not include Kazakh or Russian, and covers a limited group of languages and tasks. | Gives a possible approach to taxonomy-based representations, but local-language performance must be verified. |
| [11] Zhang et al. (2022) | Extraction of skill and knowledge phrases from English job advertisements. | SkillSpan contains 14,538 sentences and 12,629 annotated spans; compares BERT-based models and training strategies. | Domain-adapted models perform better in the tested extraction tasks. | [12] Reimers and Gurevych (2019) | Sentence embeddings for semantic comparison. | Sentence-BERT uses Siamese or triplet networks and compares sentence vectors through similarity measures. | Enables much faster semantic search than comparing every text pair with a regular BERT cross-encoder. | Performance changes between tasks; it does not measure occupational suitability. | Provides a basis for embedding-based ranking, not a proof of career fit. |
| [13] Lewis et al. (2020) | Language generation using external retrieved information. | Combines a dense retriever and a BART generator, tested on question answering, generation and fact verification. | RAG improves several tested tasks and gives more specific and factual generations than a parametric-only baseline. | Incorrect answers are still possible. The experiments are not about career plans, and the original method uses joint model training. | Supports retrieving learning and occupation information before generating a roadmap. Sources and proposed steps still need checks. |
| [14] Es et al. (2024) | Automated evaluation of RAG systems without reference answers. | RAGAS measures faithfulness, answer relevance and context relevance, tested against human pairwise judgements on WikiEval. | Accuracy in pairwise comparisons with human judgements was 0.95, 0.78 and 0.70 for the three measures, better than the tested baselines. | Uses a small general-knowledge benchmark and LLM-based judges. A good score does not confirm the practicality of a career plan. | Can support automatic grounding checks, with expert evaluation for roadmap quality. |
