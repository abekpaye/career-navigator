# Evidence of Relevance

Three semi-structured interviews were conducted to understand how students and early-career IT specialists choose directions, assess job requirements and plan their development. The findings were used to refine the initial requirements of the proposed platform.

## Interview Overview

| Interview | Participant | Main perspective |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I1 | IT student considering DevOps and Machine Learning Engineering | Career choice and assessment of current skills |
| I2 | Early-career IT specialist completing a DevOps internship | First-job preparation, CVs and learning at work |
| I3 | IT practitioner experienced in supervising interns | Junior requirements and practical career advice |

Questions were adapted to each participant's experience. I1 and I2 described their own preparation, while I3 provided a practitioner perspective.

## Key Findings

I1 found it difficult to judge whether university knowledge met workplace requirements and used vacancies and AI tools to identify skills to learn. I2 noted that technology requirements vary across companies, while I3 described overlap between IT roles. This suggests that the platform should explain documented role requirements without treating one skill list as a universal hiring standard.

I1 considered a career-match percentage especially useful. However, I3 warned that a high score could hide an essential missing requirement. The planned score will therefore describe weighted coverage of documented requirements, with matched, missing and unconfirmed information shown separately. It will not represent the probability of employment.

Roadmap expectations differed. I1 wanted clear learning steps with reasons and practical projects but valued matching more highly. I2 preferred official documentation and guidance reviewed by experienced specialists. I3 emphasized technical foundations, projects and early internship applications. These responses support prerequisite-based steps with practical outputs and reviewed resources rather than a generic list of topics.

I2 reported spending time adapting a CV for different vacancies, while I3 stressed the importance of a candidate's personal contribution to projects. I1 and I2 also identified teamwork and communication challenges. These findings support an editable profile that captures project experience and development plans that include practical and career-preparation activities, not only technologies.

## Evidence-to-Requirement Mapping

| Interview evidence | Requirement informed |
| --- | --- |
| I1 struggles to compare skills with vacancies; I2 and I3 describe variation in role requirements. | Document role requirements, show skill coverage and allow users to choose a profession independently. |
| I1 values a match score; I3 cautions against hiding essential gaps. | Provide explainable, weighted career matching with visible matched, missing and unconfirmed requirements. |
| I1 requests practical steps; I2 trusts documentation; I3 emphasizes foundations and internships. | Generate ordered roadmaps with expected outputs, prerequisites and reviewed learning resources. |
| I2 adapts CVs; I3 values evidence of personal project contributions. | Support manual profiles and CV extraction, followed by user review and editing of skills and projects. |
| Participants use separate vacancies, documentation, AI tools and personal advice. | Connect profile review, career recommendations and development planning in one workflow. |

## Limitations

Three interviews provide useful examples, but they do not represent all students or junior specialists. I3 offered an experienced practitioner's view rather than the perspective of a current beginner. The interviews did not establish whether the planned catalog of ten professions is sufficiently broad or whether multilingual support and progress tracking are useful to users. These remain project decisions to assess later. Recommendation quality and the usability of the complete workflow will be tested using a baseline comparison, expert review and user tasks.
